The Story Behind the Work

From Mesquita to the world — a journey through code, identity, and justice

Hi, I'm Louise — and this is the unfiltered version

My path to AI research wasn't planned out in advance. It's been messy, full of doubt, and shaped mostly by things that had nothing to do with Python or neural networks.

I'm a Black, queer woman from Mesquita in Baixada Fluminense — a place most people in tech have never heard of. It's also where I first learned what happens when systems don't see you: when algorithms don't account for people like you, when "objective" technology somehow manages to be anything but.

"I didn't come to AI to build cool things. I came because I got tired of being an afterthought in other people's 'innovations.'"

That's the lens I bring to this work — not just technical skill, but the lived experience of navigating spaces that weren't built with me in mind. Both show up in my research.

The Journey So Far

A few moments that shaped how I actually think about technology

The Beginning

Mesquita, Baixada Fluminense

There were no coding camps or robotics clubs growing up in Baixada. What I got instead was a close-up view of what happens when you're invisible to the people making decisions about your life.

I watched my community get reduced to crime statistics, and friends with real talent never get a chance to show it. That's where my interest in fairness started — years before I knew what an algorithm was.

2009-2013

International Relations & Finding My Voice

I started International Relations at UFSC expecting to end up in diplomacy or policy. What I actually learned was how power works: who gets heard, who gets ignored, and how systems keep producing unequal outcomes even when the people running them believe they're neutral.

That way of looking at things stuck with me. Years later, moving into tech, I realized algorithms are just policy written in code.

2014-2016

Poland, Culture, & Starting to Connect the Dots

My MA in Cultural Studies in Poland was where things started clicking. I spent two years studying how culture shapes identity and who gets to tell which stories.

At the same time, I was watching AI systems make the exact same mistakes — erasing marginalized voices, calling bias "objectivity," flattening complicated people into data points. Once I saw the parallel, I couldn't stop seeing it.

2020-2024

Falling Into Tech (Kind Of)

There was no grand plan behind any of this at first. I needed a job in 2020, and Revolut was hiring for customer support — verifying identities, running compliance checks, explaining to people why their account had been frozen. Not glamorous, but steady.

The shift happened at HSBC, where I moved into compliance and communications monitoring — watching for insider threats and policy violations across a global bank. That's where I realized security work actually suited me: not just the technical side, but understanding why certain communities need protecting, and what it means when "security" has historically looked more like surveillance than safety.

So I went looking for it on purpose. By 2022 I'd landed a cybersecurity role at EY, working in data loss prevention and compliance — GDPR, LGPD, all the alphabet-soup regulations. That job taught me something I still carry: technical solutions without ethical frameworks are just expensive ways to automate harm.

2023

The Moment It All Made Sense

Sometimes you don't choose your research topic — you stumble into something that makes you angry enough to do something about it.

For me, that was watching content moderation algorithms penalize LGBT creators and sex workers: neutral terms getting flagged as inappropriate, queer content getting shadowbanned, and everyone shrugging it off as "well, that's just the AI."

It isn't just the AI. It's human prejudice operating at scale, and once I saw that, everything I'd learned — cultural studies, policy, tech, my own life — finally pointed the same direction. I could actually do something about it.

2024-2025

Building the Future I Want to See

I finished my MSc in AI with Distinction at Essex as a Chevening/Lemann Scholar. My dissertation audited algorithmic fairness in adult content classification — in plain terms, it proved the machines were being bigots, and showed exactly how to fix it.

Beyond the dissertation, I'm working on cultural data mining and building datasets for underrepresented communities, because intersectional analysis isn't a nice-to-have here — it's a technical requirement if you want the results to actually hold up.

The best way to fix broken systems is to build better ones. I've got a list.

The Parts That Don't Fit on a Resume

🏳️‍🌈

Queer in Tech

Being openly queer in tech — especially as a Black woman from Brazil — means constantly translating yourself: explaining why certain "edge cases" matter, why content moderation that targets LGBT language isn't a bug, it's oppression with a better UI.

My queerness shows up directly in my research. When I talk about bias in classification systems, I'm talking about algorithms that would flag me.

✊🏾

Black, Brazilian, & Unapologetic

I'm from Mesquita. I'm Black. I'm Brazilian. And I refuse to minimize any of that to make people comfortable. These aren't fun facts about me — they're the reason I understand what's at stake when AI systems fail.

My community taught me resilience. My identity taught me what bias looks like in practice. My work is how I fight back — with code.

🌍

Global Experiences, Local Heart

I've lived in three countries, studied at four universities, worked across continents — but home is still Baixada, and it always will be.

That tension between global ambition and local roots keeps me grounded. I'm not trying to build AI for Silicon Valley. I'm trying to build it for the people who get left behind when tech "moves fast and breaks things."

What Drives Me

01

Justice Over Optimization

I'm not interested in squeezing out 2% more accuracy if that 2% comes at the expense of marginalized communities. Fairness is the point of this work, not a metric to optimize around.

02

Representation Isn't Enough

Being the only [fill in the blank] in the room is exhausting. I don't want to be the token. I want to build systems where people like me don't have to constantly prove we belong.

03

Community Before Career

Every door that opened for me, someone kicked down first. That's why I mentor, why I do DEI work, why I refuse to "make it" and forget where I came from. We rise together, or not at all.

So, That's Me

Messy, multidisciplinary, and maybe a little too invested in making sure algorithms don't screw over the people I love. I didn't take the traditional path into AI research, and I'm glad I didn't — this field needs people who already understand that technology isn't neutral. It's exactly as biased as the humans who build it.

My job is to make it less biased. My mission is proving that intersectional, justice-driven AI isn't optional — it's necessary.

If you want to see the actual work, check out my projects. And if you want to talk about any of this, reach out — I'm always up for a conversation about building better systems.
